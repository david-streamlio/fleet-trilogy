## Opening (3 slides)

**O1. The problem.** AI adoption means more data centers, with their power, water and carbon costs. They aren't going away, and we need them. So the question is narrower: what can *a developer* do to shrink AI's environmental impact, if anything?
- [SOURCE] One or two citable numbers for scale (e.g. IEA "Energy and AI", 2025: data-center electricity demand and its growth). Few and solid.

**O2. What I promised you** (bookend). The accepted abstract, quoted in full:
> "1-bit LLMs replace GPU matrix math with CPU additions. The theoretical energy savings are striking — 85 to 96% less per inference. Add a conditional invocation so the model only fires on anomalies, and the gap widens further. I'll walk through the math, the architecture patterns, and what it means for you."
- Spoken: "Hold me to this. I'll come back to it at the end."

**O3. The spark and the question.**
- 1-bit LLMs (BitNet b1.58, Microsoft Research, 2024) [SOURCE] suggested work normally sent to a cloud LLM might run on far smaller hardware.

- **The question:** what environmental impact is possible if phones, not data centers, did even a small part of that work?

## Section 2: The test at a glance (3 slides)

**T1. The use case, as code.** "Given this sensor data, identify the medium- and high-severity risks that impact the ETA."
- One telemetry event (532 B of JSON every 5 s) → a cheap-math gate (most readings never reach the LLM) → the LLM decides one thing: raise, hold or lower the severity, with a one-paragraph reason → only high-severity cards cross the cellular link.
- Visual: Talk 1's event JSON beside the enrichment card (`docs/TALK1-SLIDE-PLAN.md`, slides 17 and 20), the gate as an arrow between them.

**T2. How I measured.**
- **Accuracy, with the dice question:** roll two dice 100 times. You expect a distribution, not one answer: about 17 sevens, but anywhere from ~10 to 24 is normal. LLM output is sampled too, so every accuracy figure is a rate with a range. (The twist, that the die is loaded, is observation 5.)
- **Speed:** time per job (median and the slow tail), and whether the job finishes at all, not just tokens per second.
- **Power:** an inline meter on the Pi 4 (whole board, at the wall); the chip's own sensors on the Macs (500 ms samples); energy per job above idle.

**T3. The hardware spectrum.** Chart: **Fig. 1** (generation speed across platforms, with the published iPhone 17 Pro points).
- Mainstream Android stand-in → flagship Android stand-in → iPhone 17 Pro class (an M1 Mac mini matches its published speed within 9%) → newer Apple silicon (M4) → a developer laptop (M4 Max). The Raspberry Pi 4 (2019, real, wall-metered) is the dedicated-edge-box baseline.
- 17 models, 0.34-9.4B parameters, 4-bit, one llama.cpp build everywhere; 12-27B models on the bigger machines.
- Speaker note: what fits in memory: up to 4B in 4 GiB, 8-9B needs 6 GiB, 12B 8 GiB, 14B 12 GiB.

## Section 3: I assumed… it didn't (10 slides)

Every slide has the same four parts: **I assumed**, **what happened** (one number, or a chart), **why**, **so do this**.

**A1. "A small device will use less energy." It used more.** Chart: **Fig. 2** (a: energy vs latency per decision; b: CPU vs GPU on the same M1).
- **What happened:** per triage decision, the Pi 4 used ~154 J above idle (288 J at the wall); iPhone-class and newer Apple GPUs 15-20 J. On the same M1 chip, the CPU used ~4× the energy per token reading the prompt and 1.4-1.6× generating, for 3-9B models.
- **Why:** energy is power × time, and the slow device burns power for longer. On the same chip the CPU loses most when reading the prompt (~4× the GPU's energy per token) and still loses when writing the answer (1.4-1.6×).
- **Do this:** run on the accelerator (GPU/NPU), not the CPU. Compare joules per job, not watts.
- **Caveat (2026-10-05):** these per-call numbers repeat one event, so the prompt was cached. A changed prompt costs more: on the Pi 5× with my original prompt, 2.7× with the event fields last (A10).

**A2. "Thinking mode will make it more accurate." It didn't, and sometimes it never answered.** Chart: **Fig. 3** (correctness vs latency by prompt mode).
- **What happened:** thinking on vs off made no measurable accuracy difference: Qwen3-14B 72% → 78%, Qwen3-8B 44% → 47%, Qwen3.8-27B 67% → 67%, all within each other's ranges. It cost 4-11× the time per call and, on the iPhone-class M1, 4-7× the energy (Qwen3-14B 202 → 817 J). Qwen3.5-9B on the hard task never finished thinking: 36 of 36 calls truncated at 2,048 tokens, and 35 of 36 at 4,096 (128 s each, 0% correct). On the easy task, 4,096 was enough for most calls (61%).
- Capped thinking (budget forcing, 1,024 tokens; all three machines): every call finished (Qwen3.5-9B hit the cap every time but answered), accuracy within range of thinking off (e.g. Qwen3-14B 72-83% vs 72%), at 3-10× the energy and 3.5-17× the time. On the M1: Qwen3-14B 202 → 768 J; Qwen3.5-9B 99 → 960 J at 105 s.
- **Why:** a short, bounded judgment doesn't need multi-step reasoning, and some models don't know when to stop.
- **Do this:** turn thinking off for short structured tasks. If you need it, cap it, and measure the completion rate, not just accuracy.

**A3. "A capable enough model can do the whole job in one call." It couldn't.** Chart: **Fig. 5** (one bounded judgment vs the whole job, eight models, clustered ranges; `figures/fig5_task_scope.png`).
- **What happened:** asked to classify severity, decide the escalation and derive the action in one call, the small models dropped from 94-100% to 11-22%. Every part got worse: severity 53-78% right, consistency 47-53%, even the escalation 53-58%. In production's prompt mode no model passed 58%, even at 12-27B.
- **Why:** small models lose multi-step consistency first. A threshold is exact in code and approximate in a model.
- **Do this:** cheap math for classification, thresholds and lookups; the LLM for the judgment only (weighing rain, cargo and schedule pressure).

**A4. "Raw completion is the neutral default." The chat format mattered more than model size.**
- **What happened:** the same prompt in the model's own chat format, thinking off, took Qwen3.5-9B from 22% to 72% on the hard task (the M1 replicates it: 19% → 61%). Same model, same compute.
- **Why:** instruct models are trained on their template; a bare prompt is out of distribution.
- **Do this:** use the model's chat template (the runtime can apply it for you), and check it before blaming the model.
- **Prompt ablation** (M4 Max and M4 agree; filled in 2026-10-04): lookup tables + the model's own chat template lifted Phi-3.5, Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen3-14B by 36-42 points; worked examples helped Gemma-3-4B but nearly doubled the prompt; temperature 0 and grammar-computed fields added nothing measurable. On the M1, the template variant cost 9-52% more energy per call; worked examples nearly doubled the prompt yet cost −12% to +7%, because answers got shorter.

**A5. "Run each test enough times and the result is solid." The die was loaded.**
- **What happened:** at temperature 0.2 the wording changed every call, but the verdict mostly didn't: repeats of a test case agreed 80-90% of the time. The production check's 3 test cases × 6 repeats is closer to 3 data points than 18, so "18 of 18 correct" really means somewhere between 44% and 100%.
- **Why:** at low temperature a model is nearly deterministic for a given input. Repeats measure that one input.
- **Do this:** test many *different* cases, not many repeats; compute ranges per case.

**A6. "A cheaper quantization is a free win." It broke one model.**
- **What happened:** Q4_0 instead of Q4_K_M cut energy per call by 14-41% (10-36% on the M4), but Gemma-3-4B's error rate went from 0% to 30% (270 calls per format) and Llama's doubled, while the others held.
- **Why:** quantization error interacts with each model's calibration; a format's average quality says nothing about your model on your task.
- **Do this:** re-check accuracy for every model × quantization pair you ship.

**A7. "The defaults are sensible." One of them filled the memory.**
- **What happened:** with no context size set, the server reserved each model's full training context (128k tokens). That crashed a 16 GB CPU device out of memory and took ~50 GB on the 64 GB laptop, for a task that needs ~1-2k tokens. At 8k: ~6 GB, same speed and accuracy.
- Also: running several models in one benchmark session doubled latency (Phi-3.5: 0.70 vs 0.36 s), which had invalidated some earlier published numbers.
- **Why:** server defaults are tuned for maximum capability, not for your device or your benchmark.
- **Do this:** set the context size (and other memory knobs) for the device, and benchmark one model per process.

**A8. "Faster hardware is more efficient, and specs tell you how fast." Neither held.**
- **What happened:** the stand-in with the most memory bandwidth (202 GB/s) generated slower than one with less, because its older cores were the limit. The laptop running cool was 24-77% faster than when throttled, but used 30-60% *more* energy per call; it reached heavy thermal pressure within ~12 minutes.
- **Why:** speed has more than one bottleneck, and a device trades speed for efficiency as it heats up. Phones throttle too.
- **Do this:** benchmark on the target class of device, under sustained load, and report both the cool and the throttled numbers.

**A9. "I need to test accuracy on the target device." I didn't.**
- **What happened:** the iPhone-class M1 and the M4 Max laptop agreed on all 28 round-3 test cells, within their ranges; in 83% of cases every call on both machines scored the same. The Pi 4 matched the laptop within 6 points on three models.
- **Why:** same model file, same runtime, same decoding: hardware changes speed, not answers.
- **The limit (2026-10-05):** "same runtime" matters. The same model file and sampling settings through llama-cpp-python instead of llama-server moved Phi-3.5's borderline escalations from hold to lower, every call, on the M4 Max and the Pi 4 alike. So iterate on the fast machine, but with the runtime you'll ship.
- **Do this:** iterate on accuracy on the fastest machine you have (the laptop ran 14 models in 25 minutes; a Pi 4 run took ~2 hours per model), using the runtime you'll ship, then measure speed and energy on the target.

**A10. "My benchmark measured the cost of a call." It measured the cost of a repeat.** (Added 2026-10-05; data: paper Table IV, `docs/TALK2-HARDWARE-SPECTRUM-TEST-LOG.md` § "Real-stream re-measure".) Chart: `slides/charts/slide_changed_prompt.png` (Table IV: Pi 4 and M4 Max, repeated vs changed as built vs changed with the fields last).
- **What happened:** the benchmark sent the same event again, so the server read **1 of 514** prompt tokens per call; the rest came from its cache. A new event re-read **~400**, because my prompt put the event's fields before the rules. Moving them after the rules, same wording otherwise: **148**, with accuracy unchanged on llama-server (100% format, 0% wrong escalations), but not through llama-cpp-python, where the benign case tipped from hold to lower on every call. On the Pi 4 a new event cost **160 s and 772 J** with my original order, 5× the repeated 31.7 s and 154 J: reading 400 prompt tokens took 129 s, four times as long as writing the answer (31 s). Fields last: **82 s and 416 J**, about half. On the laptop GPU, 25.0 J → 18.5 J per new event (−26%), against ~15 J repeated.
- **Why:** a prompt cache reuses a prompt only up to the first token that changed since the last call. Within one incident a truck's calls repeat the prompt (it carries the trip context, not the sensor readings), so the earlier numbers hold for those calls; a new incident, a changed field, or one device serving several trucks pays the full read. On a CPU, reading the prompt is the slow part; on a GPU, generation is.
- **Do this:** put the fixed instructions first and the per-request data last, then re-check accuracy: a reorder is a prompt change, and a runtime change is too. Benchmark with inputs that change, and check what your server caches (llama-server's memory cache of earlier prompts served my recurring test events whole until I turned it off).

Speaker-note material, not slides: below ~1B parameters models echo the prompt or loop instead of answering; the same evaluation picked different models for two adjacent tasks (Phi-3.5-mini for triage, Gemma-3-4B for the spoken summary); things that didn't surprise me (faster hardware is faster; small models can't do multi-step work; generation speed roughly follows memory bandwidth).

## Section 4: Do this, not that (2 slides)

**D1. For small LLMs.**
- Give it one bounded judgment, not the whole job.
- Thinking off for short tasks; cap it if you need it.
- Use the model's chat template.
- Check every quantization and every runtime, per model.
- Set the context size for the device.
- Below ~1B parameters, expect echoing and loops, not answers.

**D2. For all LLMs.**
- Use them for what they're good at (language judgment); don't force them to be classifiers.
- Cheap math first: filter, classify and compute in code; call the model only when it adds judgment.
- Constrain the output with a grammar.
- Test many different cases, not many repeats (a benchmark that repeats one input measures only the cached-prompt cost).
- Put the fixed instructions first and the per-request data last, so the prompt cache can reuse them.
- Compare joules per job, on the accelerator, on the target device.

## Section 5: Does the thesis hold? (5 slides)

**C1. The uncomfortable result.**
- The thesis: moving AI work from data centers to devices people already own cuts AI's environmental cost. The data: on a CPU, the same task used *more* energy, not less (A1). So is the thesis wrong?
- Three framings, all shown:
  - **inside the data:** CPU inference doesn't save energy; the savings come from somewhere else;
  - **the accelerators phones already have:** the iPhone-class M1 GPU used ~20 J per triage decision;
  - **context only, clearly labeled:** a provider reported a median production text prompt at 0.24 Wh (~860 J), including data-center overhead [SOURCE: Google, Aug 2025]. That's a big model on general prompts, not our task; a small model batched on data-center GPUs would be cheaper still.

**C2. Where the savings came from.** Chart: **Fig. 4** (LLM energy per truck-day: invocation policy × device, with radio and idle reference lines).
- **Filtering first, so fewer LLM calls wherever the LLM runs:** in normal driving the gate passed 0 events in 2.3 truck-days.
- **No raw data on the network:** 9.2 MB per truck per day becomes ~4 KB per escalating incident (~2,300× less), and nothing on a normal day. How much energy that saves depends on the radio: an old LTE radio streaming every 5 s stays on all day (~92 kJ), a modern low-power modem (~2.5 kJ) much less.
- **Call once per incident,** not every 5 s: ~20× fewer calls.
- **Hardware that's already on:** a dedicated box's idle outweighs everything (the Pi 4's 3.4 W idle is 294 kJ a day). A phone that's on anyway only adds the marginal energy, which is the strongest argument *for* phones.
- **The math line:** energy = calls × joules per call. The invocation policy moved the total by 3-4 orders of magnitude; the device moved it by about one.
- Speaker note: the bars use the repeated-prompt cost per call. A once-per-incident call always meets a new prompt, so on the Pi it costs 5× more with my original prompt (2.7× with the fields last; A10); that bar is still ~5× below the gated one.

**C3. What to watch** (the paper's §VI, Table VI). Most of these failures were invisible to latency monitoring:

| Signal | Failure it caught | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Energy per decision | thinking on | 202 → 817 J per call, accuracy unchanged |
| Completion rate | thinking runaway | 36/36 calls truncated |
| Memory headroom | default context size | ~50 GB reserved, ~6 GB needed |
| Thermal state | sustained load | cool: 24-77% faster, 30-60% more energy per call |
| Correctness, per case | Q4_0 on Gemma-3-4B | errors 0% → 30%, energy −10-41% |
| Invocation rate | no gate vs gate | 17,280 vs 23 calls per truck-day |
| Prompt tokens read per call | a benchmark repeating one input | 1 vs 400 of 514 tokens; Pi 4 154 → 772 J per call |

- Speaker note: carbon per decision = energy × the grid's carbon intensity where and when the model ran.

**C4. What I promised vs. what I measured** (the bookend):

| The abstract said | What the study found | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| "1-bit LLMs replace GPU matrix math with CPU additions" | 1-bit models weren't usable on ARM yet. With today's 4-bit models the CPU went the wrong way: 1.4-4× the energy per token of the GPU on the same chip, and the Pi 4's CPU ~8× an iPhone-class GPU per decision | **Untested** for 1-bit; for today's models, the CPU is the wrong direction |
| "85 to 96% less per inference" (theoretical) | Theory counts arithmetic; a real device also pays for time and for being on (half of each Pi 4 call's wall energy is idle). A simpler 4-bit format saved 10-41% per call and broke one model's accuracy | **Directionally right, smaller in practice** |
| "Add a conditional invocation… the gap widens further" | 0 LLM calls in 2.3 truck-days of normal driving; ~23 calls instead of 17,280 events a day (~99.9% fewer); ~2,300× less data sent | **Confirmed, and the dominant saving** |

- Spoken: the 85-96% comes from Microsoft's BitNet b1.58 2B4T report: estimated arithmetic energy, against full-precision models. Ternary weights would still move ~2.8× fewer bytes than our 4-bit files, so a working 1-bit runtime could still help on phones (future work).

**C5. The answer.**
- Moving an LLM to a cheap CPU doesn't save energy by itself; it costs more.
- The savings come from the architecture around the model: filter with cheap math, call the model rarely, keep raw data off the network, and run on hardware that's already on, using its accelerator.
- Phones are fast enough (an iPhone 17 Pro ≈ an M1 for these models) and accurate enough for simple, bounded tasks. That's the slice of work they can realistically take off the data center.
- **Closing line:** "The energy savings came from not calling the model, not from making each call cheaper."
